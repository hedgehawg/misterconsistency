# CLAUDE.md

## Communication preferences

- **Acronyms: spell them out.** Err on the side of writing the full name on first use — e.g., "Liquidity Focused Collateral Treatment (LFCT)", "US Unemployment Rate (UNR)", "Canada Unemployment Rate (CAUNR)", "Fed Funds Rate Decision (FFDEC)" — unless Scott has already used the acronym himself in the conversation. This applies everywhere: chat replies, generated documents, tables, and agendas. Market tickers, exchange program terms, and regulatory shorthand especially.

## Writing style — correspondence drafted on Scott's behalf

Lessons from Scott's edits to drafts. Apply these to all emails, messages, and
other correspondence written in his voice.

1. **Cut filler that adds no information.** Phrases like "on your end,"
   "going forward," or restating context the reader already has just add
   length. If deleting a phrase changes nothing, delete it.

2. **When asking a vendor or counterparty for a date or timeline, ask for
   their "best estimate."** Repairs, builds, and shipping carry inherent
   uncertainty; demanding "the timeline" invites either silence or a made-up
   date. "Your best estimate on timeline" is less confrontational and easier
   to answer honestly.

3. **Never state facts the recipient already knows as implied pressure.**
   Constructions like "now that it's in your hands" or "as per my last email"
   read as thinly-veiled "ball's in your court" pressure. It won't catalyze
   prioritization — it just sours the tone. If they know it, don't say it.

4. **Soften requests in vendor correspondence.** "Please kindly confirm" over
   "please confirm"; "let us know" over "let me know" when writing on behalf
   of the business. Courteous and low-pressure gets better service than
   terse and transactional.
