## 00 prologue
This is the Sun and the Earth, drawn to scale: one astronomical unit, a hundred and fifty million kilometres, on the date you see in the corner. Earth is a dot on a circle nine hundred and forty million kilometres round. Travelling with it are five places where a small mass can keep Earth's year without being at Earth's distance from the Sun: the Lagrangian points. We will visit each of them. First, home.

## 01 earth
Earth, twelve thousand seven hundred and forty-two kilometres across, turning with the real hour. Everything on this voyage keeps its true size and its true distance, so most of what we meet will be smaller than a pixel until we fly in. Four hundred and eighty kilometres up, Hubble circles the globe once every ninety-four minutes. The thin gold ring beyond it is geostationary orbit, where a satellite keeps pace with the turning Earth. That ring is our first stop.

## 02a geo (at 0 s)
Geostationary orbit is not a Lagrangian point. It is the ordinary circular orbit whose period is one sidereal day: forty-two thousand one hundred and sixty-four kilometres from Earth's centre, six point six Earth radii.

## 03 L1
One and a half million kilometres sunward of Earth: the first Lagrangian point, roughly one percent of the way to the Sun and four times the Moon's distance. A spacecraft here keeps Earth's year with an uninterrupted view of the Sun, which is why the solar observatories live here: SOHO since nineteen ninety-six, Discover, ACE, Wind and Aditya L1. The point itself is a saddle. Nothing sits on it; each craft flies a loop around it and burns fuel to stay. Turn the frame with Earth and the loops appear: these are their real tracks, from JPL. Here is SOHO, nine and a half metres across its wings.

## 04 L2
Back past Earth to its shadow side, the same one and a half million kilometres out: L2. From here the Sun and the Earth stay together on one side of the sky, so a single sunshield can keep a telescope cold. James Webb orbits the point in a halo, two hundred and fifty thousand to eight hundred and thirty-two thousand kilometres from it, once every six months, with a small burn about every six weeks. Nancy Grace Roman, launched on the thirtieth of August, is on its way to join it. Here is Webb at true size: twenty-one metres, a kite of five foil layers, with the gold mirror looking out from the cold side.

## 05 L3
Now the long way round: over the Sun to the far side. L3 lies opposite Earth, beyond the Sun, permanently hidden from us. It is a saddle like L1 and L2, and it is empty. A counter-Earth could not hide here; its pull on the other planets would have given it away long ago. No spacecraft has ever been stationed here either. The crosshair marks a point, not a body.

## 06 L4
Sixty degrees ahead of Earth on its orbit: L4. Here the mathematics changes. Because the Sun outweighs Earth by far more than the twenty-five to one it requires, this point is stable: not a well but a hilltop of the effective potential, where the Coriolis force bends any drift into a loop around the point. Earth's only confirmed Trojans both live here: twenty ten T K seven, about three hundred metres across, and twenty twenty X L five, a dark rock about one point two kilometres across. No telescope has ever resolved either; what you see is a stand-in, at true size.

## 07 L5
And sixty degrees behind Earth: L5, the mirror of L4, just as stable, and empty. No Trojan has been found here. STEREO B passed by in two thousand and nine, Spitzer drifted through in twenty eleven, Hayabusa two looked in twenty seventeen and found nothing, STEREO A came by in twenty twenty. The European Space Agency plans to station Vigil here early next decade, to watch the Sun's weather coming. Our voyage ends at an empty point on the orbit of the Earth.

## 08 finale
Five places, one period. Two are hilltops that hold; three are saddles that need a steady hand. Everything you saw was at true size and true distance, on today's date. Explore freely.
